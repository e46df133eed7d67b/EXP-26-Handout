    Introductory

    To understand what philosophy is about, you must do it. So, we will try to incorporate some philosophy into our discussion of the Department of Philosophy’s two majors, the Philosophy major and the Computer Science + Philosophy major.

    Even though we offer the CS+Philosophy major, I will typically talk more about philosophy than I will about CS. I am not a computer scientist by training. Moreover, I am acting on the assumption that you are familiar with CS at Illinois via presentations from CS and Grainger advisors, as well as presentations from other CSX advisors. What is distinctive of CS+PHIL is not the mere prescence of CS, but, first, philosophy, and second, the blending of CS and philosophy into what you might think of as “computerosophy.”

    Question 1. What is the major about?

    This is a much more difficult, interesting question than you might realize. In certain disciplines, it is possible to ‘step back’ or ‘step outside’ the discipline and take a kind of Archimedean view upon it. For instance, you do not have to do chemistry upon chemistry itself – what would that even mean? – in order to know what chemistry is. (Though perhaps my STEM colleagues will correct me today.) Philosophy does not work this way. Any attempt to survey the discipline is itself an exercise in philosophy. There is no neutral territory here, and when philosophers do try to find neutral territory on this question, it is boring and unilluminating. (An example: “Philosophy is from the Greek philosophia - philia means ‘love,’ sophia means ‘wisdom,’ and hence, ‘love of wisdom.’” Yeah, great, fine, sure.) That said, I will offer some characterizations, to which I am sympathetic, of what philosophy is up to, with the caveat that not everyone in philosophy will agree.

    1. Wilfrid Sellars writes that “the aim of philosophy, abstractly formulated, is to understand how things in the broadest possible sense of the term hang together in the broadest possible sense of the term.” Philosophy involves the attempt to understand how, indeed, we have a world in the first place. (A world, you might think, is something like the totality of that which hangs together.)

    2. Ludwig Wittgenstein writes in the Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus that “philosophy is not a body of doctrine but an activity.” (Recall what I said above at the outset; I will return to this idea below as well.) Philosophy is not something you learn by going to class and letting the slides wash over you because, in a certain sense, philosophy is not something you learn in the first place. Rather, philosophy is what happens when you try to make sense of what is written on that slide. It is what you – yes, you – do when you read ‘philosophy is not a body of doctrine but an activity,’ then my gloss on it, and then think about whether that gloss on it makes sense. And note that we can shift the italics and say it’s not just something you do: it’s also something you do.

    3. Wittgenstein writes in his later work, the Philosophical Investigations, that “the aspects of things that are most important for us are hidden because of their simplicity and familiarity. (One is unable to notice something—because it is always before one’s eyes.) The real foundations of their inquiry do not strike people at all. Unless that fact has at some time struck them.—And this means: we fail to be struck by what, once seen, is most striking and powerful” (§129). Deep truths need not be, as it were, esoteric truths. Philosophy does not require you to become an oracle or, to use Nietzsche’s cheeky phrase, a “telephone of the Beyond.” Everything you’re looking for might just be in front of your face.

    Question 2. Why might a student be interested?

    The influential British philosopher Bernard Williams quips in an interview that one of his colleagues liked to say that, historically, philosophy tends to be done for two sorts of reasons: curiosity or salvation. In my experience, there is something to this. “Salvation” these days usually does not look like Christian redemption: the salvation that is sought by students usually involves admission to law school, grad school, the workforce, or something like that. Among philosophers, there are people who do it for salvation, and their concern is not about getting a job or something: they instead might think that, through philosophy, you can leave behind ignorance and come to know things of various sorts. There are still people who do philosophy simply because they’re curious and it’s interesting. Curiosity might be expressed in all sorts of ways: returning to the idea of exploring the world, you might want to understand how the world of first nature - think the purely physical world - constitutes a world in the first place, but you might also have this question about the social world. And you might also think not about how the world is, but instead, how it ought to be You also do not have to think about the world: you can think about how society ought to be, for instance, and indeed, you can think about how you ought to be. How one ought to live seems not to be just any other question.

    Question 3. What are some courses that introduce the major?

    Any of them. I usually just encourage students to take what they find interesting. Our 100- and 200-level courses typically do not have prerequisites or restrictions; we don’t offer a ton at the 300-level but these have no prerequisites anyway; 400-level courses typically have a prerequisite of one philosophy course. (Even so, it is not true that any one 100-level course prepares you just as well as any other for any 400-level course. If you have taken an introductory ethics course, such as PHIL 105, you will not be ready to take a 400-level metaphysics course, such as PHIL 426.) Instructors also have significant academic freedom in designing and delivering their courses. An introductory philosophy course, such as PHIL 100 or PHIL 101, taught by one instructor might not share any readings or assignments in common with the same course, taught by another instructor, and yet both can be recognizable as introductory philosophy courses.

    For these sorts of reasons, the catalog “sample sequences” for Philosophy is useless, in principle. It is more useful for CS+Philosophy students, since CS courses seem to follow a sequence (124, 128, etc.). But with philosophy courses, it is just as useless for them as it is for Philosophy students. The idea isn’t that it could’ve been useful but failed to be so, perhaps due to the person who put it together: rather, the idea is that thinking about things in terms of “usefulness” was a mistake from the start. (As Wittgenstein puts the thought elsewhere in the Investigations, at that point “the decisive movement in the conjuring trick has been made, and it was the very one that seemed to us quite innocent” (§308).)

    Why “any of them?” My thinking is that philosophy either does not have a foundation or, if it does, it has foundations in the way a spider web does, namely, by having many. And much like a spider web, you cannot isolate a single “strand,” as it were, and study it in isolation from the others: if you go for one strand, you get the whole web. (Why not? That single strand would constitute an Archimedean point, but I have denied you can find such a point in philosophy.) In turn, there is a sense in which, when you do philosophy, you are always already behind: your own thinking needs a set of presuppositions in order to proceed in the first place, but those presuppositions are themselves philosophically contentious. So when students take a philosophy course and they feel like they are behind, I find myself telling them, more and more, that it is in the nature of the activity itself that you are behind. (Though—let’s be real—too much Thirsty Thursday doesn’t exactly help either.)

    Question 4. What are some career options?

    Our students do a lot of reading and writing, and they are well-prepared for careers involving these skills. Our CS+PHIL students, in addition, have a strong background in computer science. I discuss this in greater detail in Question 6.

    Question 5. What are some opportunities for involvement and research?

    There are several. Regarding involvement, there are several RSOs associated with our majors in some form or another – Philosophy Club, AI Alignment, the Techne Undergraduate Philosophy Journal, and I am working with some students to reactivate Illinois’s chapter of an honor society in philosophy, Phi Sigma Tau. The department holds several colloquia, featuring visiting speakers, each semester, and these are open to the public and listed on our Events page.

    Additionally, the Department is home to the Illinois Forum on Human Flourishing in a Digital Age. It is interdisciplinary, but with a strong philosophy slant. The Forum hosts visiting speakers for a lecture series, a new short Lunch Talk series, and several reading groups. The Forum is also home to the Illinois Luddite Society. Lastly, the Forum is working on establishing and cementing in place some undergraduate fellowships.

    As for research, philosophy typically does not operate on the model of labs. (There are some rare exceptions in areas such as experimental philosophy, but to my knowledge, we do not offer any of these exceptions these days, though we do have a faculty member with serious interests in x-phi.) Undergraduate research in philosophy typically involves doing an independent study (PHIL 390) or a thesis (PHIL 492). Well-prepared undergraduate students also occasionally take 500-level graduate seminars.

    Question 6. What are some transferable skills your majors gain, and where do they work after college?

    Students cultivate their reading and writing skills, along with their ability to explain, assess, and elaborate upon arguments. They develop their imaginations. Lots of Philosophy students are interested in law school—close to 50% of the Philosophy majors have the Pre-Law designation, based on a report I ran recently in FA26. Others are interested in graduate school, and others enter the workforce. CS+Philosophy students typically have career interests which are similar to those of other CS and CSX students, and my understanding is that they are able to find the same sorts of work after college. The Illini Success Survey has some more information.

    In general, I take my title seriously and literally, and I do not pretend to have much to say about career stuff. My background is academic and I have virtually no experience with the non-academic job market—the only non-academic job I ever really applied to is my current one. I refer students to LAS Career Services for career questions. They are the experts. I am not.

    I know that, at this point, I am supposed to say something like “yes, I know I need to learn,” but I think that’s false. Leaving aside the fact that there are others both in LAS and campus who will do a better job, I do not want to participate in the project of downplaying academics in favor of extracurriculars and careerist aspirations. This project does exist, whether you like to admit it or not; I think we all participate in it, whether we like it or not. It has, plausibly, existed for a long time, and so you might say “that’s nothing new,” but whatever else an old problem is, it is a problem.

    Further, philosophy is one of the humanities, and the point of the humanities is not something like “to churn out resumes and, as it just so happens, human beings who have those resumes.” Nor—hot take—is the point to turn out truths of the sort you can use to compile a theory, which you can then write down in a book; because philosophy is not a body of doctrine but an activity, a thought I have returned to a few times here, and while you can write down a body of doctrine and leave it somewhere for a rainy day, you cannot do so with an activity. For these reasons, AI cannot eliminate philosophy (the closest it can get is by eliminating the records of philosophy or prompts to philosophy in the form of papers, books, etc.), because that would involve the elimination of human thoughtfulness itself. (We should recognize, though, just how little consolation this offers. Fine, AI can’t eliminate thoughtfulness, but it can eliminate the human beings who do the thinking. And it can do things which are the faint echo of such elimination. If good ’ol Chat can do pharmeceutical and biological research, it can also do research on chemical and biological weapons.)

    A university education in philosophy, then, in my view, is about becoming a certain sort of human being. It is hard to say what that sort of human being is like: students are all different, and yet the ones who have educated themselves appropriately nevertheless seem to have something in common. At a first pass, I would say they are supposed to be thoughtful. In any event, becoming a certain sort of human being is an activity. Indeed, being a certain sort of human being is an activity.

    When we take an appropriately broader view of the point of education, we could perhaps say that students gain not just skills, but certain sorts of virtues.

    I think it is more helpful to think about the sorts of virtues a human being cultivates in their education. True, as part of that cultivation, they will also cultivate certain sorts of skills, which they can use in different areas of life. But unless they undergo the severance procedure, they are also going to have to, as it were, transfer themselves between these different areas of life. They should then cultivate not just skills, but virtues, so they live a life they can take seriously.
